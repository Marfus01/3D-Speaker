## Point-by-Point Responses to AE & Reviewers
### Associate Editor
"The paper presents a multimodal framework that combines pre-trained deep learning models with a hidden Markov model to jointly infer character identities and align them with names in TV-show videos. Compared to a benchmark method, the authors showed that their method achieved improved performance on benchmark datasets."

"Reviewers have some major concerns regarding its novelty, comparisons to benchmark methods, and the lack of detailed ablation studies. The integration of name matching into audio–visual diarization is a promising direction, the authors did not sufficiently compare to existing AVD literature, overlook key baselines and voice-overlap challenges, and offer limited statistical or algorithmic novelty."
Answer:
In terms of novelty, we would like to respectfully clarify that our contribution is not merely combining HMMs with Deep Learning (DL), but specifically improving deep learning models' performance on challenging tasksand give a quantification of uncertainty to the predictions of black-box deep learning models in dynamic, multimodal environments. While purely data-centric approaches ( supervised fine-tuning on labeled data) are effective to some extent, they are expensive and often unsustainable given the rapid evolution of real-world data. Our framework demonstrates that integrating domain-specific probabilistic structures (e.g., temporal turn-taking dynamics, cross-modal dependencies, prior knowledge on unreliability of predictions from deep learning models) via an HMM can correct deep learning errors in low-resource/noisy scenarios without requiring massive retraining. Furthermore, the probabilistic nature of HMM allows us to quantify the uncertainty of the predictions made by deep learning models.

Regarding comparisons to benchmark methods, we apologize for the initial oversight of Audio-Visual Diarization (AVD) literature and baselines. We have extensively revised Section 2.4 to include the definition of SD, typical SD system pipeline, and recent advancements on audio-visual speaker diarization(AVD). In revised Section 5.2. We introduced CAM++ (SOTA speaker embedding) and compared our method against strong SD/AVD baselines, including Spectral Clustering (SC), VBx, Pairwise Constrained Clustering (PCC) based on active speaker detection and k-means clustering with visual centers. As shown in Table 3, our method consistently outperforms these baselines.

For the lack of detailed ablation studies, we have conducted additional ablation studies to evaluate the contributions of different components of our framework. Specifically, we have evaluated the performance of our method without the HMM component, as well as the impact of face-recognition in key-frames and different covariates(such as active speaker recognition, audio segment length) in the HMM. Please refer to the revised Section S4 in the Supplementary Material for details. The results show that HMM plays a crucial role in enhancing the performance of deep learning models, and each covariate contributes to the overall performance, especially face-recognition in key-frames.

In terms of handling voice-overlap challenges, In our work, considering the availability of subtitles in TV plays, we segmented the audio according to subtitle time stamps instead of running VAD and segmentation steps additionally. This operation ensures that each audio segment aligns with a line. In TV plays, due to the nature of dialogues, characters typically speak one at a time, so our assumption of "each audio segment A_{i,t} corresponds to a single speaker S_{i,t}" generally holds. In practice, we had attempted to strictly remove overlapping speech regions in each audio segment according to predictions from a state-of-the-art overlap detection model(pyannote/segmentation-3.0). However, this operation resulted in overly fragmented audio segments and slightly degraded the speaker recognition accuracy. Therefore, we maintained the original preprocessing strategy in the revised manuscript. For more general senarios, when subtitles are not available, our method can be easily extended by replacing the "Subtitle Segmentation" module with a standard "VAD + Segmentation" module to obtain speaker-homogeneous segments. Under this setting, our method shares the same limitation on the handling overlapping speech with other clustering-based speaker diarization methods. We have discussed this limitation in Section S1.2 of the revised manuscript.

"The experimental evaluation would also benefit from more rigorous metrics to validate claims."
Answer:
In the representation learning part in Section 5.3, we use Equal Error Rate( EER) as the metric to evaluate the quality of learned speaker embeddings, which is a standard metric in speaker recognition tasks. For more rigorous evaluation, we also included additional metrics such as Minimum Detection Cost Function(minDCF) and AUC in the revised Section S7.3 of the Supplementary Material. The results consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of self-supervised representation learning in improving speaker embedding quality. If there are other specific metrics you would like to recommend, please let us know, and we will be happy to consider them.

"2. Unrealistic assumptions about audio segments
2.1. The method assumes each audio segment A_{i,t} corresponds to a single speaker S_{i,t}, yet real-world broadcasts frequently contain overlapping speech."
Answer:
Our current framework indeed assumes each audio segment corresponds to a single speaker. As we mentioned in section 3.1, considering the availability of subtitles in TV plays, we segmented the audio according to subtitle time stamps instead of running VAD and segmentation steps additionally. This operation ensures that each audio segment aligns with a line. In TV plays, due to the nature of dialogues, characters typically speak one at a time, so our assumption generally holds. In practice, we conducted an additional experiment using pyannote/segmentation-3.0, a state-of-the-art model for overlap detection. In this experiment, we first use pyannote/segmentation-3.0 to predict number of speakers over time, then we refined the subtitle-based segments by figuring out the longest continuous single-speaker intervals within each audio segment. As the following table shows, strictly removing "overlapping" regions resulted in overly fragmented audio segments and slightly degraded the speaker recognition accuracy. We attribute this to three reasons:
- Duration Trade-off: Strict overlap removal fragmented the audio into short segments, rom which embedding extractors (like CAM++) cannot extract reliable identity features.
- Existence of Laughter Tracks: Laughter tracks are commonly used as background in TV shows. However, current overlap detection models can't tell them from actual overlapping speech, leading to unnecessary removal of valid speech segments.
- Dominant Speaker Energy: In TV series, even when overlap occurs (e.g., laughter or brief interruptions), the target speaker typically dominates the audio energy.
Therefore, we maintained the original preprocessing strategy in the revised manuscript. For more general senarios, we acknowledge the importance of handling overlapping speech in speaker diarization. When subtitles are not available, our method can be easily extended by replacing the "Subtitle Segmentation" module with a standard "VAD + Segmentation" module to obtain speaker-homogeneous segments and applying the proposed HMM framework on these segments.

"2.2. No voice-activity or speaker-embedding segmentation is performed to handle speaker overlap, calling into question performance in natural settings."
Answer:
Thanks for the comment. As you pointed out, a typical speaker diarization systems follows "clustering-based" paradigm, which often involve four key steps: (1) voice activity detection (VAD) to identify speech segments, (2) speech segmentation to divide the audio into speaker-homogeneous segments, (3) speaker embedding extraction to represent each segment, and (4) unsupervised clustering to group segments by speaker identity. Among these steps, VAD and speech segmentation are more like preprocessing steps to provide speaker-homogeneous segments(not handling speaker overlap exactly). In our work, considering the availability of subtitles in TV plays, we replace the first two steps with subtitle-based segmentation. This allows us to isolate and focus on the core challenges in SD under complex acoustic conditions. For more general senarios, as mentioned above, our method can be easily extended by replacing the "Subtitle Segmentation" module with a standard "VAD + Segmentation" module to obtain speaker-homogeneous segments. Under this setting, our method shares the same limitation on the handling overlapping speech with other clustering-based speaker diarization methods. To be specific, Missed Speech(MS) and False Alarm(FA) terms in Diarization Error Rate (DER) metric are not negligible. We have discussed this limitation in Section S1.2 of the revised manuscript.

"3. Limited technical novelty
3.1. The approach combines existing machine-learning models with an HMM framework, but does not introduce fundamentally new algorithms."
Answer:
While HMM is a classic statistical model and pre-trained deep learning models are widely used in various applications, the core idea of our work is to show by integrating appropriate statistical modeling with deep learning, we can leverage simple yet useful domain information that are often ignored by deep learning models to improve deep learning models' performance on challenging tasks and give a quantification of uncertainty to the predictions of black-box deep learning models. To mitigate this distribution shift, the prevailing paradigm focuses on continuous data collection, large-scale re-labeling, and frequent model re-training (e.g., via active learning or supervised fine-tuning). While effective to some extent, these data-centric approaches are inherently expensive, time-consuming, and often unsustainable given the rapid evolution of real-world data. Moreover, although deep learning models are powerful tools to make predictions, they often lack interpretability and uncertainty quantification, making it difficult to assess the reliability of their predictions. We believe exploring better solutions for both of these issues are important research topics of statistical community in the deep learning era. In this work, we attempt to address these issues by integrating statistical modeling with deep learning. To be specific, by incorporating useful imformation(e.g., cross-modal dependencies, temporal dynamics, prior knowledge on unreliability of predictions from deep learning models) into the HMM, our framework can effectively correct some errors made by deep learning models under challenging scenarios(e.g., short&noisy audio segments, low quality face images). Furthermore, the probabilistic nature of HMM allows us to quantify the uncertainty of the predictions made by deep learning models.

"3.2. Core priors—“speakers usually appear on screen” and “speakers rarely say their own names aloud”—are intuitive but lack empirical validation and may not generalize across genres or formats."
Answer:
Thank you for the comment. In TV shows video, due to the nature of dialogues and the third-person camera perspective, the two priors generally hold. For the first prior, as we mentioned in section 3.1 and 3.2, we conducted empirical analysis on our datasets to support these priors. Specifically, we randomly sampled 500 audio segments each from both datasets, manually annotated speaker labels and face presence in key-frames, and calculated the percentage of segments where the speaker's face appears in the key-frames. The results show that in the BigBang dataset, the speaker's face appears in key-frames for 86.4% of the segments, and in the LoveFamily dataset, it appears for 93.4% of the segments. These statistics validate the effectiveness of this prior in our datasets. In more general senarios, since facial activities and lip motion are highly related to speech, leveraging visual information about faces in the senario(e.g., detect and recognize active speakers' faces) to improve speaker diarization performance is a mainstream research topic in AVD literature. These works are based on the same prior in fact.

For the second prior, we also conducted empirical analysis on our datasets in a similar way. Specifically, we randomly sampled another 2,000 audio segments each from both datasets, manually annotated speaker labels and the names mentioned in the segments, and calculated the percentage of segments where the speaker says names of each character. As the following figure shows, in both datasets, the percentage of segments where the speaker says names of himself/herself is very low(generally less than 5%), validating the effectiveness of this prior in our datasets. Furthermore, to make our method more robust to potential violations of this prior in more general senerios, we upgraded previous optimization-based mapping algorithm with a LLM-based mapping strategy. Please refer to the revised Section S5.2 in the Supplementary Material for details.

Sincerely thankS for YOUR constructive comments.
