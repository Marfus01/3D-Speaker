## Point-by-Point Responses to AE & Reviewers
### Associate Editor
"The paper presents a multimodal framework that combines pre-trained deep learning models with a hidden Markov model to jointly infer character identities and align them with names in TV-show videos. Compared to a benchmark method, the authors showed that their method achieved improved performance on benchmark datasets."

"Reviewers have some major concerns regarding its novelty, comparisons to benchmark methods, and the lack of detailed ablation studies. The integration of name matching into audio–visual diarization is a promising direction, the authors did not sufficiently compare to existing AVD literature, overlook key baselines and voice-overlap challenges, and offer limited statistical or algorithmic novelty."
Answer:
In terms of novelty, while Hidden Markov Model(HMM) is a classic statistical model and pre-trained deep learning models are widely used in various applications, the core idea of our work is to show when integrating appropriate statistical modeling with deep learning, we can leverage simple yet useful domain information that are often ignored by deep learning models to improve deep learning models' performance on challenging tasks and give a quantification of uncertainty to the predictions of black-box deep learning models. To mitigate this distribution shift, the prevailing paradigm focuses on continuous data collection, large-scale re-labeling, and frequent model re-training (e.g., via active learning or supervised fine-tuning). While effective to some extent, these data-centric approaches are inherently expensive, time-consuming, and often unsustainable given the rapid evolution of real-world data. Moreover, although deep learning models are powerful tools to make predictions, they often lack interpretability and uncertainty quantification, making it difficult to assess the reliability of their predictions. We believe exploring better solutions for both of these issues are important research topics of statistical community in the deep learning era. In this work, we attempt to address these issues by integrating statistical modeling with deep learning. To specific, by incorporating useful imformation(e.g., face recognition in key-frames, audio segment length, active speaker recognition) into the HMM, our framework can effectively correct some errors made by deep learning models under challenging scenarios(e.g., short&noisy audio segments, low quality face images). Furthermore, the probabilistic nature of HMM allows us to quantify the uncertainty of the predictions made by deep learning models.

Regarding comparisons to benchmark methods, especially audio-visual diarization (AVD) baselines, by mistake we overlooked some key AVD literature and baselines in the initial submission. We sincerely apologize for this oversight. In the revised manuscript, we have thoroughly reviewed the audio-only and audio-visual speaker diarization(SD) literature and included relevant citations and discussions in Section 2.4, and added comparisons to various speaker diarization baselines. In particular, we select CAM++ as the pre-trained speaker embedding model, which is a state-of-the-art(SOTA) model for speaker embedding extraction. In clustering step, for audio-only speaker diarization baselines, we include Spectral Clustering(SC) and Bayesian HMM clustering of x-vector sequences (VBx). For audio-visual speaker diarization baselines, we include Pairwise Constrained Clustering(PCC) based on active speaker recognition and k-means clustering with visual centers. Please refer to the revised Section 5.2 and Table 3 for details. The results show that our method consistently outperforms these baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of our approach.

For the lack of detailed ablation studies, we have conducted additional ablation studies to evaluate the contributions of different components of our framework. Specifically, we have evaluated the performance of our method without the HMM component, as well as the impact of face-recognition in key-frames and different covariates(such as active speaker recognition, audio segment length) in the HMM. Please refer to the revised Section S4 in the Supplementary Material for details. The results show that HMM plays a crucial role in enhancing the performance of deep learning models, and each covariate contributes to the overall performance, especially face-recognition in key-frames.

In terms of handling voice-overlap challenges, we would like to fist clarify that the audio-part task we consider in this work, speaker diarization with segmentation provided by subtitles, is slightly different from the standard speaker diarization task considered in AVD literature. A typical speaker diarization systems often involve four key steps: (1) voice activity detection (VAD) to identify speech segments, (2) speech segmentation to divide the audio into speaker-homogeneous segments, (3) speaker embedding extraction to represent each segment, and (4) unsupervised clustering to group segments by speaker identity. In our work, considering the availability of subtitles in TV plays, we segmented the audio according to subtitle time stamps instead of running VAD and segmentation steps additionally. This operation ensures that each audio segment aligns with a line. In TV plays, due to the nature of dialogues, characters typically speak one at a time, so our assumption of "each audio segment A_{i,t} corresponds to a single speaker S_{i,t}" generally holds. In practice, we conducted an additional experiment using pyannote/segmentation-3.0, a state-of-the-art model for overlap detection. In this experiment, we refined the subtitle-based segments by figuring out the longest continuous single-speaker intervals within each subtitle segment. As the following table shows, strictly removing "overlapping" regions slightly degraded the speaker recognition accuracy. We attribute this to three reasons:
- Dominant Speaker Energy: In TV series, even when overlap occurs (e.g., laughter or brief interruptions), the target speaker typically dominates the audio energy. Modern speaker embedding extractors (like CAM++) proved robust enough to capture the target identity despite minor interference.
- Duration Trade-off: Strict overlap removal fragmented the audio into short segments. As widely recognized in speaker recognition, embeddings extracted from extremely short segments are less reliable than those from slightly noisy but longer segments.
- Existence of Laughter Tracks: Laughter tracks are commonly used as background in TV shows. However, current overlap detection models can't tell them from actual overlapping speech, leading to unnecessary removal of valid speech segments.
In conclusion, in the specific context of TV shows with subtitles, our assumption is valid and effective. Therefore, we maintained the original preprocessing strategy in the revised manuscript. This allows us to isolate and focus the latter two steps, which are the core challenges in SD under complex acoustic conditions.

For more general senarios, we acknowledge the importance of handling overlapping speech in speaker diarization. As previously mentioned, our proposed method follows typical "Clustering-based" speaker diarization paradigm in general. Hence, when subtitles are not available, we can replace the "Subtitle Segmentation" module with a standard "VAD + Segmentation" module to obtain speaker-homogeneous segments. Under this setting, our method shares the same limitation on the handling overlapping speech with other clustering-based speaker diarization methods. To be specific, Missed Speech(MS) and False Alarm(FA) terms in Diarization Error Rate (DER) metric are not negligible. We have discussed this limitation in Section S1.2 of the revised manuscript.

"The experimental evaluation would also benefit from more rigorous metrics to validate claims."
Answer:
In the representation learning part in Section 5.3, we use Equal Error Rate( EER) as the metric to evaluate the quality of learned speaker embeddings, which is a standard metric in speaker recognition tasks. We rewrote the relevant part in section S7.3 of the Supplementary Material to clarify this point. For more rigorous evaluation, we also included additional metrics such as Minimum Detection Cost Function(minDCF) and AUC in the revised Section S7.3 of the Supplementary Material. The results consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of self-supervised representation learning in improving speaker embedding quality. If there are other specific metrics you would like to suggest, please let us know, and we will be happy to consider them.

"The paper proposes a task that is essentially an extension of Audio–Visual Diarization (AVD) with the addition of name matching, but this framing raises several concerns:"
Answer:
Sorry for the confusion. We would like to clarify the difference between our task and standard SD/AVD tasks first, which we have added in Section 1 of the revised manuscript. In breif, SD task s the task of answering the question “who spoke when” by partitioning an audio stream into segments with timestamps and corresponding speaker labels. A typical speaker diarization system employs a multi-stage framework, including VAD, segmentation, speaker embedding extraction, and clustering. Audio-visual diarization (AVD) further leverages visual information(e.g., face activities and lip motion) to improve the performance of speaker diarization, especially under complex acoustic conditions. In our work, considering the availability of subtitles in TV plays, we didn't run VAD and segmentation steps, and focus on AVD task with segmentation provided by subtitles, which allows us to isolate and focus on the core challenges in SD under complex acoustic conditions.

In terms of name matching, our method only relies on name entity recognition(NER) results in two minor steps of initialization: determining the number of main characters and mapping cluster labels to their corresponding character names. To better illustrate our framework, we emphasize this point in the revised Section 3.2. Moreover, to better compare with existing AVD literature, we treated the cluster-to-name mapping step as a post-processing step after speaker diarization in the revised manuscript, and directly evaluated the speaker diarization performance before name mapping. Please refer to the revised Section 5.2 and Table 3 for details.

"3.2. Core priors—“speakers usually appear on screen” and “speakers rarely say their own names aloud”—are intuitive but lack empirical validation and may not generalize across genres or formats."
Answer:
Thank you for the comment. In TV shows video, due to the nature of dialogues and the third-person camera perspective, the two priors generally hold. For the first prior, as we mentioned in section 3.1 and 3.2, we conducted empirical analysis on our datasets to validate these priors. Specifically, we randomly sampled 500 audio segments each from both datasets, manually annotated speaker labels and face presence in key-frames, and calculated the percentage of segments where the speaker's face appears in the key-frames. The results show that in the BigBang dataset, the speaker's face appears in key-frames for 86.4% of the segments, and in the LoveFamily dataset, it appears for 93.4% of the segments. These statistics validate the effectiveness of this prior in our datasets. In more general senarios, since facial activities and lip motion are highly related to speech, it is a mainstream research topic in audio-visual speaker diarization literature to leverage visual information(e.g., detect and recognize active speakers' faces) to improve speaker diarization performance. These works are based on the same prior in fact.

For the second prior, we also conducted empirical analysis on our datasets in a similar way. Specifically, we randomly sampled another 2,000 audio segments each from both datasets, manually annotated speaker labels and the names mentioned in the segments, and calculated the percentage of segments where the speaker says names of each character. As the following figure shows, in both datasets, the percentage of segments where the speaker says names of himself/herself is very low(generally less than 5%), validating the effectiveness of this prior in our datasets. In addition, we we treated the cluster-to-name mapping step as a post-processing step after speaker diarization in the revised manuscript, and replaced our previous optimization-based mapping algorithm with a LLM-based mapping strategy to make our method more robust to potential violations of this prior in more general senerios. Please refer to the revised Section S5.2 in the Supplementary Material for details.
